You may have been tricked: here's what to do, in order

Don't stay alone with this. These scams are very well made and catch everyone. The faster you act, the less damage there is.
  1. Call your bank straight away, using the number on the back of your card or on your statements (never a number received in a message or given by the caller). Ask them to block the card and report any transactions you don't recognise.
  2. If someone “took control” of your computer: switch it off, then follow my guide “After a virus” before signing in to your accounts again.
  3. If you gave a password or a code received by text: change your email password, then your bank's, from another device if possible (how to do it).
  4. Get help: in France, 17Cyber (17cyber.gouv.fr) and cybermalveillance.gouv.fr guide you according to your situation and explain how to report it to the police (both in French).
  5. Tell someone close to you, so you're not alone and so they don't become the next target.

A doubt about a call or a message: the right reflexes

  1. Hang up, or don't reply. Your bank, Microsoft, the police or the tax office never ask for a code, a password or a transfer by phone or message.
  2. Call back yourself the organisation or the person, using the number you already know (on the back of the card, in your contacts). A “grandchild in trouble”? Call them directly on their usual number.
  3. Don't click the link in an unexpected text or email (parcel, fine, blocked account). Type the website's address yourself instead.
  4. Report it: in France, forward scam texts to 33700 (free), and report dubious websites or emails at internet-signalement.gouv.fr (PHAROS).
  5. Fewer unwanted calls: register your number on bloctel.gouv.fr (free, French numbers) and turn on your phone's call filter (“Silence Unknown Callers” on iPhone, “Caller ID & spam” on Android).
A sentence to stick on the fridge: “A code received by text is never given to anyone.”

A virus or a dubious program on the computer

Don't panic: it's common, and it can be fixed. If Microsoft Defender found something, it has usually already put it aside.

  1. Run a Microsoft Defender offline scan (the steps).
  2. Check the installed programs and the browser, without removing anything you don't know.
  3. Change your important passwords, starting with your email.

Securing a Windows computer

No need to buy an antivirus: Windows 11 is well protected if it's set up properly. The most useful points:

  • Just one antivirus: Microsoft Defender, already included. Two antiviruses get in each other's way.
  • Automatic updates, left switched on.
  • A code when the computer starts and a protected Wi-Fi.
  • Two-step verification on your email and your bank.
  • An automatic backup of your documents and photos.

Not losing your photos and documents

  1. The simple rule: your photos should exist in two places. If they're only on the phone or the computer, they can disappear with it.
  2. The easiest: an automatic online backup. On iPhone, iCloud Photos. On Android, Google Photos. On a Windows computer, OneDrive or Google Drive.
  3. As well, if you like: an external hard drive, plugged in once a month.
  4. Check once that it works: open a backed-up photo from another device.

Keeping in touch with family

  1. Choose just one app, the one the family already uses: most often WhatsApp (messages, photos, video calls with up to 32 people), or FaceTime if everyone has an iPhone or an iPad.
  2. Install it together and add the family's contacts with their photo: it's easier to call someone you recognise.
  3. Create a family group where the grandchildren send photos. That's often what makes people want to get started.
  4. A regular time (for example a video call on Sundays) gently builds the habit.
Tip: put the app's icon in large size on the first screen, and remove the others from that screen.

A first device for a beginner

  • Senior smartphone (for example the Doro range, about €150 to €250): big buttons and icons, loud sound, emergency button.
  • Or an ordinary smartphone in “simple mode” (about €150 to €250 for an entry-level Android): useful if WhatsApp will be needed later.
  • Start with just one thing: making and answering calls. The rest will come later.
Before buying: try it in the shop. How readable the screen is and the size of the buttons matter more than the features.

A device for someone very elderly or frail

  • Flip phone with big buttons (about €60 to €120): simple, sturdy, with an emergency button that alerts family.
  • Simplified tablet on subscription (for example LiNote): it answers the family's video calls by itself and shows the photos sent by relatives. Nothing to handle.
  • If the risk of falling worries you, also look at personal alarms.

A device for someone fairly confident

  • Refurbished iPhone (from about €250): very simple, FaceTime, adjustable text size and zoom. “Assistive Access” mode can reduce the screen to the essentials.
  • Entry-level Android smartphone (about €150 to €250): good value for money, with a “simple mode” in the settings.
  • Tablet (entry-level iPad, about €400 to €500): the big screen is ideal for video calls and photos.
Good to know: a refurbished device with a guarantee costs much less than a new one and works very well.

Everyday health

  • Medical appointments: in France, Doctolib. You can add your parent as a “relative” in your own account and manage their appointments from a distance.
  • Medicines: reminders with the iPhone's Health app or a free app such as MyTherapy. For more simplicity, a connected pill box alerts relatives if a dose is missed.
  • Video consultation: useful for a repeat prescription or a small problem. Do a test video call before the first appointment.
The right order: start with just one tool, try it for two weeks, then add another if needed.

Living alone at home, safely

  • Classic personal alarm (téléassistance): a pendant or bracelet with a button, linked to a 24-hour monitoring centre. About €20 to €25 a month.
  • Automatic fall detector: raises the alarm even if the person can't press the button. About €25 to €35 a month.
  • No subscription: a phone with an SOS button or a watch with fall detection, which alert relatives directly (but not a monitoring centre).
Financial help in France: a 50% tax credit on the alarm subscription (under home services), and the APA (personal autonomy allowance) can cover part of it. The département or your mutuelle sometimes have offers: ask at the town hall or the local information point for older people.
Before signing: read the cancellation terms (notice period, minimum duration), especially in case of moving house or going into a care home.

Supporting someone who struggles, or refuses

  1. Start from what they miss, not from the device: “Would you like to see photos of the little ones more often?” is more tempting than “You should have a smartphone”.
  2. Just one thing a week. Week 1: switching on and off. Week 2: making a call. Week 3: sending a photo.
  3. The 3-2-1 method: you show 3 times, you guide 2 times, the person tries alone 1 time.
  4. Let them keep their hands on the device, and write the steps on a card, in large print.
  5. If they get stuck: take a break, do something they already know how to do, and pick it up another day.
Avoid: “It's easy!”. Say instead: “I understand, it's confusing for everyone at first.”

Free help near you

  • Digital advisers and France Services centres: trained people, free of charge, to learn how to use a phone or a computer, or to do official paperwork online. The map of locations is on societenumerique.gouv.fr. Your town hall or library can also point you in the right direction.
  • Les Bons Clics (lesbonsclics.fr): free, simple tutorials for beginners (in French).
  • Emmaüs Connect (emmaus-connect.org): support and refurbished devices at low prices.

A parent who can no longer manage everything alone

French law has several measures, from the lightest to the most protective. The least restrictive possible is always chosen.

  • Mandat de protection future (future protection mandate): the person chooses in advance who will look after them. It must be done while they can still decide.
  • Habilitation familiale (family authorisation): a relative (child, spouse, brother or sister…) is authorised by the judge to act for them. Often the simplest solution when the family gets on well.
  • Curatelle and tutelle (guardianship): decided by the judge, for broader help.
These are general pointers. For your own situation, see service-public.fr or ask a notaire. To report abuse of an older person, call 3977.
